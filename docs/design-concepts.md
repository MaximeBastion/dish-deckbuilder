# Design Concepts

This document captures the current design intent. It should guide rules and
card changes, but it is not a rulebook.

## Primary Emotional Target

The main feeling to maximize:

> The satisfaction of presenting a meal you are proud of.

The game should produce moments where a player looks at their table before
scoring and thinks: "This is a good meal."

## Pitch

Open your restaurant, build your deck, learn recipes, prepare your best meal,
and win over the judge.

## Design Pillars

### Dishes Are The Center

Dishes should not be dead victory cards. They should matter during the game by:

- scoring immediately through Taste and Favor;
- filling meal slots;
- carrying faction tags;
- enabling course bonuses;
- creating visible meal narratives;
- sometimes having play effects.

Economy cards exist to support dishes, not replace them as the main source of
fun. Because Time represents learning over multiple days, it can be spent on
dishes, techniques, and long-term support cards.

Open: final name for long-term support/passive cards.

### Meals Should Be Visible

The course structure should make the player's current turn legible:

- Appetizer
- Main
- Dessert
- Extra

This structure creates pride and resonance. For now, course labels are not hard
limits: players may present duplicate courses. Completion bonuses should still
reward variety strongly enough that a complete meal remains desirable.

Allowing duplicate courses reduces feel-bad hands and lets factions create
opulence or excess, while completion bonuses keep balanced meals relevant.

### Short Games With Fast Identity

Target game length is roughly 5 to 20 minutes. The game should end before it
overstays its welcome and leave players wanting another run with a different
market.

To support short games:

- starting decks should be small;
- learned cards should matter quickly;
- Peel and Upgrade effects can be aggressive;
- market cards should create identity after a few purchases;
- the judge track should be short enough to create pressure.

### Interaction Creates Tension

Interaction is desirable, including occasional brutal effects, but average
interaction should not erase the core meal-presentation emotion.

Good interaction creates stories more often than it prevents stories.

Examples of desirable interaction:

- reduce Taste this turn;
- disrupt an attachment;
- add a Status;
- punish a specific course/tag;
- steal a recipe after it has already scored.

Occasional wild cards are allowed. The target is roughly one memorable wild
event every other short game, not constant denial.

### Resonance Over Abstraction

Players should often be able to guess a card's role from its name and flavor.
The rules meaning should feel like a natural extension of the food concept.

Examples:

- Time is spent learning recipes, improving technique, and developing the
  restaurant over multiple days.
- Taste is how impressive a dish is.
- Favor is the judge being convinced.
- Peel removes unwanted cards from the deck.
- Distraction is a blank Status.

The restaurant setting exists to justify learning and long-term growth, but the
primary fantasy should still be immediate: the chef/player presents dishes and
takes direct actions.

### Bilingual-Friendly Vocabulary

The game should work in both English and French. Rules keywords should be simple
and stable across both languages where possible.

Current core terms:

| Concept | English | French direction |
|---|---|---|
| Resource | Time | Temps |
| Scoring value | Taste | Gout |
| Judge progress | Favor | Faveur |
| Remove from deck | Peel | Eplucher |
| Dead status | Distraction | Distraction |

Card names may be localized more freely than rules keywords.

## Faction Identities

Each faction should have both a flavor identity and a mechanical relationship
to the course structure.

### French

Emotional promise: "My one dish is flawless."

Mechanical identity:

- best individual dishes;
- perfect plating;
- judge superiority;
- fewer dishes, higher quality;
- precise pairings.

Course relationship:

- rewards the best single dish;
- rewards refined pairings;
- can care about exactly one exceptional dish, even when duplicate courses are
  allowed.

Tags:

- Meat
- Seafood
- Cheese

### American

Emotional promise: "I brought way too much food."

Mechanical identity:

- cheap dishes;
- opulence;
- extra food;
- duplicate slots;
- draw and proactive pressure;
- unhealthy power boosts.

Course relationship:

- rewards extra Mains or excess dishes;
- turns duplicates into value.

Tags:

- Meat
- Fried
- Tomato

### Japanese

Emotional promise: "Everything is exactly in its place."

Mechanical identity:

- precision;
- ordering;
- filtering;
- Peel;
- clean complete meals;
- consistency over brute force.

Course relationship:

- rewards exact Appetizer/Main/Dessert;
- rewards no waste or no Extra;
- manipulates deck order to complete the correct meal.

Tags:

- Seafood
- Fried
- Rice

### Italian

Emotional promise: "The meal keeps coming."

Mechanical identity:

- warmth;
- comeback energy;
- chaining dishes;
- dramatic turns;
- actions and reactions;
- long meal momentum.

Course relationship:

- finds missing dishes;
- presents extra courses when behind;
- extends the meal through seconds, drinks, or family-style abundance.

Tags:

- Cheese
- Tomato
- Rice

## Strict Tag Graph

Each faction has exactly 3 tags, and each tag is shared by exactly 2 factions.
This creates a bridge for every faction pair:

| Pair | Shared Tag |
|---|---|
| French + American | Meat |
| French + Japanese | Seafood |
| French + Italian | Cheese |
| American + Japanese | Fried |
| American + Italian | Tomato |
| Japanese + Italian | Rice |

For now, avoid adding extra dish tags. If a card concept needs a new tag, first
ask whether it can use one of the six existing tags or whether the strict graph
should be revised.

## Current Design Risks

- Too much course restriction can make duplicate dishes feel dead.
- Too much economy can make dishes secondary.
- Too much interaction can undermine meal pride.
- Too many tags or factions too early can hide whether the core loop works.
- Too many clever systems can make a short game feel cramped.

The first prototype should answer one question above all:

> Is it satisfying to learn dishes, assemble a meal, and move the judge?
